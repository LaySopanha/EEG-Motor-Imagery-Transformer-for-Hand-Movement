1. Problem Statement

Across ASEAN, an estimated 60 million people live with physical disabilities affecting limb function — caused by strokes, traumatic injuries, congenital conditions, or progressive neurodegenerative diseases. According to the World Health Organization (2023), Southeast Asia carries one of the highest per-capita burdens of stroke-related disability globally, with countries like Cambodia, Myanmar, Indonesia, and the Philippines reporting inadequate access to rehabilitation services.

For individuals who have lost hand or arm function, regaining voluntary control — whether through prosthetics or assistive robotic systems — is transformative for independence and quality of life. Yet the two dominant assistive technologies available today both fall short in the ASEAN context:

Myoelectric (EMG) Prosthetics
Require intact residual muscle tissue. Inaccessible to individuals with high-level amputations or paralysis. Advanced models cost USD 20,000–100,000 — prohibitively expensive for most ASEAN households.
Traditional Rehabilitation
Demands intensive, months-long physical therapy. Severely under-resourced across rural ASEAN. Low therapist-to-patient ratios (often 1:500+) in countries like Cambodia and Laos.

The core challenge is the absence of an affordable, non-invasive, and intelligent interface between the human brain and assistive hardware — one that does not depend on physical muscle activity, works with standard consumer-grade equipment, and can be deployed across ASEAN's diverse healthcare infrastructure.

Core Problem: People with upper-limb disabilities in ASEAN lack access to affordable, brain-controlled assistive devices because existing BCI solutions are expensive, hardware-dependent, or clinically impractical for low-resource settings.

This gap is further compounded by the lack of local AI expertise in BCI research, limited computational resources in public hospitals, and language barriers in accessing international rehabilitation tools. Without a region-specific, low-cost BCI solution, millions of ASEAN citizens remain unable to regain independence.

2. Target Beneficiary Group

Primary Beneficiaries

  • Upper-limb amputees and stroke survivors in ASEAN — particularly in lower-middle income countries (Cambodia, Myanmar, Laos, Indonesia) where affordable prosthetics are out of reach.
  • Individuals with spinal cord injuries or ALS who retain cognitive function but have lost motor control, and who could use a non-muscular interface to operate devices.
  • Rehabilitation patients undergoing neurological recovery, who could use Brain2Hand as a real-time biofeedback tool to re-train motor intention pathways.

Secondary Beneficiaries

  • Rehabilitation clinics and hospitals across ASEAN seeking cost-effective neurofeedback and BCI tools.
  • Assistive technology NGOs and disability organisations operating in Southeast Asia.
  • AI and neuroscience researchers in ASEAN universities who lack accessible BCI frameworks built on regionally supported infrastructure (e.g., Huawei Ascend NPUs).
  • General public and students who will benefit from AI literacy activities conducted as part of the outreach plan.

3. Use of Artificial Intelligence

3.1 Overview

Brain2Hand decodes motor intention directly from electroencephalography (EEG) — the electrical activity generated by the brain as it imagines hand movements — using a purpose-built deep learning model. No physical movement is required. The system translates thought into action.

3.2 Model Architecture: HybridBrainTransformer

The core AI model is a novel Hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture designed specifically for the temporal and spatial characteristics of EEG signals:

INPUT
Raw EEG: (Batch × 641 time-steps × 64 channels)
↓
STAGE 1 — Spatial-Temporal CNN
Conv1D (64→128 ch, kernel=15) · BatchNorm · ELU · MaxPool · Dropout(0.3)
↓
STAGE 2 — Transformer Encoder
d_model=128 · 4 attention heads · FFN=512 · 2 encoder layers · captures long-range dependencies
↓
STAGE 3 — Classification Head
Global Average Pool → Dense(128→64) → ELU → Dropout → Dense(64→2)
↓
OUTPUT
Left Hand / Right Hand motor imagery prediction · ~0.8M parameters

Figure 2 — HybridBrainTransformer architecture: three-stage processing pipeline

The CNN stage acts as a spatial feature extractor, learning local patterns and reducing dimensionality across the 64 EEG electrode channels. The Transformer stage applies multi-head self-attention to capture long-range temporal dependencies across the 4-second EEG trial window — a capability that classical CNNs and RNNs lack. This combination is specifically suited to EEG data, where meaningful signal patterns span both localised electrode regions and extended time scales.

3.3 Data Pipeline & Training

  • Dataset: PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery Dataset — 109 subjects, 64 channels at 160 Hz, publicly available.
  • Preprocessing: 8–30 Hz bandpass filter (isolating Mu and Beta motor rhythms), 4-second epoch extraction, Z-score normalisation.
  • Data Augmentation: Gaussian noise injection and temporal shifting (3× dataset expansion) to improve generalisation.
  • Hyperparameter Optimisation: Automated Bayesian search using Optuna (TPE sampler, median pruning) to tune learning rate, number of transformer layers, dropout, and batch size.
  • Framework: Built entirely on MindSpore 2.7.1 — optimised for Huawei Ascend NPUs with a target accuracy of ≥ 90%.

3.4 Why AI is the Right Approach

Traditional signal processing methods (bandpower features + LDA classifiers) achieve 70–75% accuracy on motor imagery tasks and are fragile under real-world noise conditions. Deep learning — specifically the CNN-Transformer hybrid — learns representations directly from raw signal data, adapts to individual neurological variability, and achieves state-of-the-art performance without hand-crafted features. It also enables real-time inference (<50ms latency), which is essential for responsive prosthetic control.

4. Feasibility

4.1 Technical Feasibility

Brain2Hand is already implemented and functional. The complete codebase (~1,000 lines of Python) encompasses model definition, data loading, training, evaluation, hyperparameter tuning, and a live web dashboard — all built on open-source libraries (MindSpore, MNE-Python, Optuna, Streamlit).

Development Environment
Runs on a standard laptop CPU for development. Scales to Huawei Ascend / NVIDIA H200 GPUs for full 109-subject training (15–30 minutes vs. 6–8 hours on CPU).
Hardware Requirements (End-User)
A consumer-grade EEG headset (e.g., OpenBCI Cyton, ~USD 500) and any computer capable of running Python 3.8+. No medical-grade hardware needed.

4.2 Data Feasibility

The PhysioNet dataset is freely available with no licensing restrictions for research and education. It provides 109 subjects — sufficient for robust generalisation. The system also includes a mock data mode that allows offline demonstration without an internet connection, enabling deployment in low-connectivity settings.

4.3 Team Capability

The development team has demonstrated competence across EEG signal processing (MNE-Python), deep learning model design (MindSpore), automated ML (Optuna), and full-stack deployment (Streamlit dashboard). The project has already passed multiple implementation milestones, including GPU-accelerated training and a real-time control interface.

4.4 Risk Mitigation

  • Inter-subject variability: Addressed through data augmentation, Z-score normalisation, and training on 109 subjects.
  • Real-world noise: Gaussian noise augmentation during training explicitly prepares the model for non-laboratory conditions.
  • Hardware cost: The software stack is hardware-agnostic; lower-cost EEG headsets (USD 200–500) can be substituted as they reach the ASEAN market.
  • Regulatory pathway: Initial deployment targets research and educational use; clinical application would follow standard medical device review processes.

5. Scalability & Impact

5.1 Technical Scalability

The Brain2Hand architecture is modular and cloud-ready. Three deployment modes are built in: Laptop (2 subjects, for prototyping), Cloud (25 subjects, competition-scale), and Full (109 subjects, maximum accuracy). Training on Huawei Ascend NPUs provides a 20–30× speedup, reducing the barrier for institutions with access to cloud compute but limited local hardware.

5.2 Geographic Scalability across ASEAN

Brain2Hand is designed to work with standard international datasets (PhysioNet) and consumer hardware available across ASEAN. The system's web dashboard (Streamlit) can be hosted on any cloud provider and accessed through a browser — enabling remote use in rural hospitals and community health centres in Cambodia, Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, and beyond.

5.3 Application Expansion

  • Wheelchair navigation: Extending the binary (left/right) classifier to a multi-class model for directional wheelchair control.
  • Communication aids: Adapting the model for eye-movement or P300-based communication for locked-in syndrome patients.
  • Neurorehabilitation biofeedback: Using real-time motor intention decoding as a physiotherapy training signal to accelerate post-stroke recovery.
  • Educational neuroscience: Deploying as a classroom BCI demonstration tool in ASEAN universities to democratise neuroscience education.

5.4 Positive Impact

Community Level
Restores independence for individuals with upper-limb disabilities. Reduces caregiver burden. Applicable immediately in Cambodia and across ASEAN with existing affordable EEG headsets.
National / Regional Level
Open-source BCI framework for ASEAN researchers. Supports national disability inclusion policies. Demonstrates ASEAN AI capability in medically impactful domains.
